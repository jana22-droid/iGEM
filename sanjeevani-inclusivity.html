Inclusivity

Who gets to take part in science, who benefits from it, and whose harm gets counted? Our inclusion work began with those questions and four groups: persons with disabilities, students facing a barrier to school, women in mining, and tribal and rural learners.

Inclusion is not an outreach theme we added after the science. It is part of how we understand the people affected by mining, who gets access to scientific education, whose experiences are heard, and who can take part in decisions about environmental restoration.

Our approach

Who can take part, who benefits, who is heard

Access, dignity, representation and participation: we asked these questions of every activity, and they led us to four groups whose barriers we could do something about.

For our moss-based mine-waste project, responsible development cannot be judged only by laboratory performance. We also ask whether information is understandable, whether affected communities can take part, whether risks and benefits are communicated openly, and whether people who are often overlooked can reach the opportunities that environmental and industrial change creates.

Inclusivity is not simply reaching more people. It is making participation meaningful, information accessible, and decision-making more attentive to people whose needs and perspectives may otherwise be missed.

Disability inclusion

Swabhiman and Dr. Sruti Mohapatra: harm that is not counted

Dr. Sruti Mohapatra, founder of Swabhiman and a disability-rights advocate, agreed with our concerns for persons with disabilities and for victims of occupational hazards in mining, and noted that only death counts are taken into account.

Her point changed the questions we ask. If only deaths are counted, then injuries, long illness and disability caused by mining work drop out of the record, and so do the people living with them. It led us to ask whether persons with disabilities can reach the information, facilities, schemes, training and jobs connected to mining and related industries.

Inclusion needs more than acknowledging disability: it asks whether places, communications and systems are accessible in practice. A move toward safer and more sustainable mining should not leave persons with disabilities out of workforce planning, consultation, public communication or access to services.

With Dr. Sruti Mohapatra and Swabhiman

Work in progress with MACUA

With MACUA, we are planning or carrying out work on the accessibility of mining facilities, information and schemes for persons with disabilities, alongside case studies of mine tailings and work on mining careers and hazards. These are commitments: we will report outcomes only when they are complete and documented.

Still open

We have not yet turned Dr. Mohapatra’s point into a concrete change in how Sanjeevani measures impact. It is one of the open questions on our Human Practices page.

Menstrual health

Menstrual hygiene kits with Odisha Rising Foundation

When a barrier to school attendance was identified, we collaborated with Odisha Rising Foundation, which provided menstrual hygiene kits, and our team distributed them.

Menstrual health is part of dignity and well-being. When people cannot get the menstrual products they need, it can become a barrier to taking part, in school and beyond; the kits address that barrier directly.

Privacy comes first. Any figure about reach should rest on distribution records, and no one who received a kit should be identifiable on this page.

Gender

Women in mining, and on our team

The women on our team went into the mines and inspected for themselves how the tailings are managed, in a sector where Indian law long restricted women’s work.

Until 2019, the Mines Act, 1952 barred women from being employed in any part of a mine below ground, and above ground outside the hours of 6 am to 7 pm.1 A 2019 notification of the Ministry of Labour and Employment opened below-ground technical, supervisory and managerial work to women during the day, and above-ground work, including in opencast mines, at night, with their written consent and safeguards.2

Our wider conversations about women in mining asked how gender shapes experiences of safety, access, representation and opportunity. Mining communities and workplaces are not all alike, and policies and technologies should be judged with different lived experiences in mind.

Our conversation with the journalist Sandhya Ravishankar added a media perspective: journalism influences which environmental and social issues reach the public, how affected communities are represented, and whose voices are heard. Her method of verifying facts also changed how we handle claims (Human Practices, Chapter 3).

Tribal and rural learners

Science that reaches tribal and rural students

Our education programmes in Paderu, Kalahandi and Ghodegaon and with KISS brought synthetic biology to students who have few chances to meet a research team.

Access to scientific knowledge is uneven. In the Paderu ITDA division, the district’s Tribal Welfare Department records a literacy rate of 32.53%, and 24.77% for females.3 That is the setting our Paderu programme worked in, and the people we met there knew things about their land that no paper had told us.

Three things helped us widen access: going in person as well as online; working through the agencies and schools that students already belong to; and a name, Sanjeevani, that people can say and remember.

Phase 3 in Paderu, a planned biotechnology laboratory and new curricula, aims to move from access to participation: students doing synthetic biology themselves, including for iGEM.

For others

Make inclusion measurable

Inclusion work is easy to describe and hard to evidence. These are the records we recommend keeping, so that others can check the work and build on it.

  • The number and age range of people reached, where consent and records permit.
  • Session topics, language and communication formats used.
  • Feedback from participants, educators and community partners.
  • Accessibility measures taken, and barriers identified.
  • Menstrual-kit distribution totals, without identifying anyone who received a kit.
  • Specific recommendations from disability-rights and women’s-rights advocates.
  • The status of each planned activity (planned, under way or completed), with evidence for each outcome.

Avoid treating communities as all alike, or describing them only in terms of disadvantage. Where appropriate, record their knowledge, priorities, feedback and contributions to the project, and get consent before publishing photographs, quotations or identifiable personal information.
