Our approach

How we teach, and what we listen for

We go to learners who have few chances to meet a research team, explain the science in terms that matter to them, and treat every session as a chance to learn from them too.

For us, inclusive science communication means explaining ideas in ways that are understandable and relevant, and encouraging curiosity rather than assuming specialist knowledge. We do not present synthetic biology as the answer to every problem. We show what it is, what our team is trying to do with it, and what we do not yet know.

Three principles shaped our sessions:

  • Go to the learners. We taught online and in students’ own districts and schools.
  • Make it relevant. We connect environmental science and synthetic biology to the land, water and living things around the students.
  • Listen as well as teach. What people told us in Paderu changed our own project (below).

Even our name came from this

D.K. Balaji, IAS, District Collector of Krishna district, suggested we call ourselves Sanjeevani because we would be working with tribal communities: a name people can say and remember, rather than a coined name such as ‘Mosscraft’. The story is on our Human Practices page.

Programme 1

ITDA Paderu: online, on the ground, and a laboratory to come

Our largest programme ran with the Integrated Tribal Development Agency (ITDA), Paderu, in three phases. It reached more than 7,000 students online and more than 70,000 school students on the ground, and it changed our own project.

The setting

Paderu is the headquarters of Alluri Sitharama Raju district in Andhra Pradesh, and the whole district comes under the jurisdiction of ITDA Paderu.1 Its 11 scheduled-area mandals are home to about 6.26 lakh people from Scheduled Tribes, 94% of the population.2 The district’s Tribal Welfare Department runs Ashram schools, which are residential from Class 3 to Class 10, and 667 primary schools for Classes 1 to 5, which it describes as single-teacher schools with about 20 students each.2

D.K. Balaji, IAS connected us with ITDA Paderu. We then worked with the agency in three phases.

What Paderu taught us

We went to Paderu to teach, and we were taught. Local people and health officials told us that arsenic from the soil itself reaches people through the rice they grow, and that some people do not take the treatment offered to them because they do not trust the medicine.

That question took us to Dr. Sudhakar Srivastava of Banaras Hindu University, who works on biochar that reduces arsenic in rice, and from that conversation came the idea of turning our harvested moss into biochar. The full story is in Chapters 4 and 6 of our Human Practices page, and the plan itself is on our Proposed Implementation page.

What we can and cannot say

  • We have not measured arsenic in Paderu ourselves. We report what we were told, and by whom.
  • Our reach figures count students in our sessions; they are not evidence of what each student learnt.

Thank you

We credit this programme to ITDA Paderu and to the IAS officers who made it possible, Aditya Verma and Sri Pooja Tirumani.

Programme 2

KISS and Voices for Nature, Bhubaneswar

With the Kalinga Institute of Social Sciences (KISS) and Voices for Nature, we brought environmental awareness and science communication to students outside mainstream research networks.

Access to environmental knowledge should not be restricted to students who are already connected to research or science networks. Our sessions made space to discuss biodiversity, environmental responsibility and the relationship between communities and their surroundings.

We also ran synthetic biology outreach linked to KIIT International School, aimed at science literacy and awareness of innovation.

Programme 3

Kalahandi, Odisha

Odisha Rising Foundation connected us with students in Kalahandi district, where all 13 blocks are associated with mining, deforestation and industrial presence.

Kalahandi district is divided into 13 blocks across two sub-divisions.3 Our outreach and camp there brought environmental and scientific topics to learners with varied educational backgrounds, in a region where access to educational resources and locally relevant information matters.

Our collaboration with Odisha Rising Foundation also included menstrual hygiene kits, which our team distributed; that work is on our Inclusivity page.

For others

Build on our work

Our Paderu programme can be reused by any team that wants to reach students far from a research institute. Here is the model, and the records we recommend keeping.

The model, step by step

  1. Find the right partner. A district administration or tribal development agency can open its schools to you. Ours was ITDA Paderu, reached through a district collector.
  2. Start online. Show students your institute and your laboratories, and teach with slides. Our online phase reached more than 7,000 students.
  3. Then go in person. Teach in the schools, and meet the people who know the place: residents, health officials, college staff. Ask about their problems, not only about yours.
  4. Leave something that lasts. Work with the administration towards a laboratory and a curriculum, so that students can go on doing science after you leave.
  5. Choose words people can use. Even a team name matters. Ours was chosen to be easy to say and remember.

Records to keep

  • The number and age range of students reached, where consent and records permit, keeping a network’s total reach separate from the students you engage directly.
  • Session topics, language and communication formats.
  • Feedback from participants, educators and community partners, and what you changed because of it.
  • Accessibility measures taken, and barriers identified.
  • Consent before publishing photographs, quotations or identifiable personal information, especially of minors.

Avoid treating communities as all alike, or describing them only in terms of disadvantage. Record their knowledge, priorities and contributions too: in Paderu, the people we met knew things about their land that no paper had told us.
