Proposed Implementation

Our moss is meant to pull heavy metals out of polluted land. This page sets out who would use Sanjeevani and how, what would happen to the metal-laden moss after harvest, what must be true before any of it leaves the lab, and where we go next.

Nothing on this page has been tested in the field. Every number here is either a benchmark from the literature or a target we still have to measure, and we label which is which.

Who it is for

Who would use Sanjeevani, and how

The first users would be the owners of metal-polluted land who pay for clean-up. The biochar made from the harvested moss would then serve buyers who need cheap metal sorbents or recovered metals.

How it would be used: eight steps

  1. 1Survey the site: map metals and their levels.
  2. 2Establish the moss on the polluted ground.
  3. 3Monitor metal uptake through the growing season.
  4. 4Harvest and bag the biomass as hazardous waste.
  5. 5Pyrolyse it in a local unit and capture the off-gas.
  6. 6Test every batch: leaching, metal content, surface area.
  7. 7Route the char: sorbent, metal recovery or soil use.
  8. 8Hand the land back with before-and-after soil data.

Any field use would first need regulatory approval (Safety and regulation). None of our work so far releases moss into the environment.

What our Human Practices added to every step

  • Who is legally responsible for the site, and what happens if the operator disappears?
  • Have the people who live and work there been informed, and given a say?
  • Who could be exposed at each step, and through which pathway?
  • Is the clean-up adding to a company’s obligations, or being used to excuse them?

These questions came from our legal and health panels (Human Practices, Chapter 2). We do not have full answers yet; any honest deployment would have to answer them first.

The people a deployment would need

Land owners (mines, industry, municipalities); pollution regulators; the communities and farmers who live nearby; pyrolysis partners; metal recyclers; and testing laboratories.

After the harvest

Why the harvested moss should become biochar

Remediation that leaves behind a pile of metal-laden moss only moves the pollution; it does not finish the job.

Toxic metals in soil are not a local problem: an estimated 14 to 17% of the world’s cropland exceeds agricultural thresholds for at least one toxic metal.1 Our moss is meant to draw such metals out of the soil and into its tissue. That creates a second problem: the harvested moss is then a concentrated, hazardous waste. So we asked what would happen if the end of our remediation pipeline produced something useful.

  1. 1Metal-polluted land threatens food and health.
  2. 2Moss phytoremediation moves the metals from soil into biomass.
  3. 3The usual fates of that biomass are poor: landfill keeps the hazard, and open burning releases carbon as CO₂ and can volatilise metals.2 On-site burning is still the common fate of biomass from pioneer plants on degraded land.3
  4. 4Slow pyrolysis turns the biomass into biochar, a stable carbon solid. For contaminated biomass, pyrolysis cuts the volume and lowers the mobility and bioavailability of the metals left in the char.4
  5. 5The char becomes a product: a metal sorbent, a recovery route for valuable metals, or, if it is safe, a soil amendment. In a circular bioeconomy, one process’s waste is the next one’s input.2

Why biochar and not another material: it is made from waste, needs no new infrastructure to apply, and can be tuned through feedstock, temperature and activation.5 Making activated carbon takes about 97 MJ/kg, against about 6.1 MJ/kg for biochar, so biochar is the lower-energy sorbent.2

Where the idea came from

A question about arsenic in rice, which we brought back from Paderu, took us to Dr. Sudhakar Srivastava of Banaras Hindu University, whose group works on biochar that reduces arsenic in rice.6 Talking with him gave us the idea of turning our harvested moss into biochar. We then built the plan on this page from four biochar studies and discussed it with him (Human Practices, Chapter 6).

Honest status

None of the four biochar studies this plan draws on used moss. They used straw, sawdust, a fern and a seagrass. Every number on this page is a benchmark from another feedstock, and data on moss is the first thing we must generate.

Making biochar

How we would make it, and what we would make

We would dry the harvested moss and slow-pyrolyse it in an oxygen-free retort at 400 to 500 °C. Phosphoric-acid activation is an optional step that gives sorbent-grade char without pushing the temperature up.

Process steps, to adapt once we test moss

  1. Harvest and dry. Wash soil off the moss with deionised water, air-dry, oven-dry at about 70 °C and chop to about 1 mm, as in the fern protocol of Mai et al.3 Weigh it and measure its metal content: this is the starting point of the metal mass balance.
  2. Optional acid impregnation (sorbent grade only). Stir the dry biomass in phosphoric acid, then dry it. Muratore et al. used 20 g of biomass in 300 mL of solution containing 41.28 mL of 85% H₃PO₄, for 6 h at 85 °C, then dried it at 110 °C for 12 h.7
  3. Slow pyrolysis under nitrogen. Seal the biomass in a steel retort, flush it with N₂, heat at 7 to 10 °C/min and hold for 1 h. Mai et al. used 10 °C/min with 3 NL/min N₂;3 Muratore et al. used 475 °C, 7 °C/min and 160 mL/min N₂.7
  4. Capture the off-gas. Syngas and bio-oil can heat the next batch; reusing waste heat and charging a gate fee for biowaste can bring the net production cost close to zero (Maroušek et al. 2020, cited in Zhao et al.).5 A filter or scrubber must catch volatile metals such as cadmium.4
  5. Cool under nitrogen. Let the char cool to room temperature before opening the retort; hot char can catch fire in air.
  6. Wash and dry (sorbent grade). Wash with hot water until neutral, then dry at 110 °C.7 Collect the acidic rinse water and neutralise it before disposal.
  7. Characterise and leach-test before any batch leaves the lab (table below).

The trade-off we expect

The fern study reached its highest surface area, 701 m²/g, at 800 °C under N₂, and recommends 700 to 800 °C.3 But our feedstock is contaminated, and at that temperature the cadmium our moss captured would leave as vapour.4 Acid activation is the way out: Muratore et al. reached 324 m²/g at 475 °C,7 in the same range as the fern char at 600 °C (387 m²/g).3 So we expect to keep the temperature low and still make a porous char. For moss, this is a hypothesis still to test.

Design rules from the studies

  • Match the char to the pollutant. Low-temperature chars keep more oxygen groups and suit inorganic pollutants such as metals; high-temperature chars suit organic pollutants.2
  • Mind the pH. The activated Posidonia char had a point of zero charge of 4.1, so it is negatively charged at pH 5 and attracts metal cations; the raw char (point of zero charge 9.2) was positively charged and took up only a quarter to a third as much. At pH 3, uptake fell to about a third of its pH 5 value.7
  • Mind competing salts. In 0.1 M NaNO₃ uptake dropped, for Dy by about half. Real effluents are salty, so test in them.7
  • Plan for single use, then recovery. Because capacity collapses after one cycle, the sorbent should be a one-pass product whose spent form feeds the recovery stream.7
  • Watch fine particles. Highly charged chars can repel or attract soil colloids, and fine particles can be lost; the soil product should be granulated.3

Every batch is tested and routed

The leaching limit itself comes from the regulator; we would set it before the first batch is tested.

Safety and regulation

Safe by design, and honest about what is unknown

Harvested moss is hazardous waste until proven otherwise, and pyrolysis is also the step that ends the life of the moss.

  • Hazardous until proven otherwise. India’s Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules, 2016 classify heavy-metal-bearing wastes as hazardous. We would bag, label and store harvested moss as hazardous waste, and check local rules for transporting it.
  • Pyrolysis doubles as biocontainment. No living cells survive 400 to 500 °C in an oxygen-free retort, so if our moss is engineered, pyrolysis is also the step that ends its life. The plan harvests the moss before it can spread by spores or fragments.
  • Scrub the off-gas. Cadmium volatilises during pyrolysis.4
  • No char on farmland without a leaching test. Heavy metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons may persist in biochar.2 Our soil product is for non-food land only.
  • Approval before any release. In India, the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee, under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, appraises proposals to release genetically engineered organisms into the environment, including experimental field trials, under the Rules, 1989 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.8 State pollution control boards would also decide whether the char and its leachate are acceptable.
  • Lab safety. Handle phosphoric acid with gloves, goggles and a fume hood. Pyrolysis gas contains carbon monoxide: burn it or vent it safely, never into the lab.

Open challenges

  • The best sorbent in Muratore et al. lost about 80% of its capacity after one reuse cycle.7
  • Real effluents are salty: 0.1 M NaNO₃ halved Dy uptake in the same study.7
  • We do not yet know moss biomass yield per hectare or its metal load at harvest; both set the scale of everything on this page.
  • In some jurisdictions, char made from hazardous waste may itself be classed as hazardous.

Life-cycle assessment

How we would test whether biochar is really better

Our life-cycle assessment would compare two fates for one tonne of dry, metal-laden moss: pyrolysis to biochar, against secure landfill or open burning.

  • Question. Does turning harvested moss into biochar lower the climate and toxicity burden of moss phytoremediation, compared with landfilling or burning the biomass?
  • Functional unit. 1 t of dry moss biomass at harvest (or 1 ha of land remediated, once we have field data).
  • Boundary. Cradle to grave: growing and deploying the moss, harvest, drying, pyrolysis, optional activation, use of the char, and the end of life of the spent char.
  • Method. ISO 14040 and 14044; a free tool such as openLCA is enough for a screening LCA.

A useful signal: biochar made from a ‘problem’ biomass already sells. India’s Varaha makes biochar from the invasive shrub Prosopis juliflora in Gujarat, and in January 2025 agreed to deliver 100,000 tonnes of carbon-removal credits to Google by 2030.11 Our moss would be a problem biomass too; the difference is the metals, which is why our first market would be metal capture, not carbon credits.

Where the money would come from

Our model would be paid for by the clean-up fee land owners already face, so the char products would only need to cover drying, pyrolysis, testing and transport. Carbon credits are upside, not the base case.

Margin per tonne of dry moss = clean-up fee + (char yield × char price) + revenue from recovered metals + carbon credits, minus the costs of drying, pyrolysis, activation, testing, transport and scrubbing

Each term would be filled from lab data and interviews. Char yield from pyrolysis runs 25 to 63% of feedstock mass (Windeatt et al. 2014, cited in Zhao et al.), so one tonne of dry moss would give roughly a quarter to two-thirds of a tonne of char.5

Pricing

  • Price per gram of metal removed, not per kilogram of char. A cheaper char that holds less metal can still lose. Our break-even price against activated carbon would be: price of moss char = price of activated carbon × (metal uptake of moss char ÷ metal uptake of activated carbon), with uptake in mg per g, measured in the buyer’s own effluent.
  • Sell the sorbent with a take-back. The best benchmark sorbent lost about 80% of its capacity after one reuse,7 so we would sell it as single-use and take spent char back for metal recovery.
  • Price the clean-up below dig-and-dump per hectare, and show that the land stays in place.

Going to market in India

  1. Start with effluent producers. Electroplating, battery and e-waste units need sorbents now and buy by the kilogram; land-remediation contracts take longer to close.
  2. Fund pilots through CSR. Under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, a company with a net worth of ₹500 crore or more, a turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more, or a net profit of ₹5 crore or more must spend at least 2% of its average net profits of the three preceding financial years on CSR.14 Schedule VII lists ‘maintaining quality of soil, air and water’ among the activities that count.15
  3. Rent pyrolysis capacity before building it. India already has industrial biochar producers, such as Varaha in Gujarat;11 partnering avoids capital cost in the pilot phase.
  4. Bring regulators in early. State pollution control boards decide whether the char and its leachate are acceptable.
  5. Add carbon credits last. Demand is strong: biochar made up 93% of durable carbon removal contracted in the first quarter of 2026, a record quarter of 2.3 million tonnes.13 But buyers want clean, certified char, so this fits our soil-grade stream only.

Questions we still need customers to answer

  • What do you pay today to dispose of one tonne of contaminated plant waste?
  • What sorbent do you use now, and what does it cost per kilogram?
  • Which metal limits does your regulator apply to soil inputs and to treated water?
  • Would you accept a slower clean-up if it cost less and left the land usable?

Roadmap

Four phases, three gates

We are in phase 1. Each phase must pass a gate before the next begins, and we will set dates once the lab results are in.

At lab scale, batches of a few hundred grams would be pyrolysed in a sealed steel box inside a muffle furnace. A pilot would use a larger batch retort with its own condenser, scrubber and gas burner, near a test site. At full scale, a continuous kiln at a regional hub would take moss from several sites, with burner heat drying the incoming moss.

Why small and local: small biochar units can create rural jobs, manage waste and be linked in closed loops where one process’s waste is another’s input.2 For us, local units would also mean that contaminated biomass travels short distances.

Future prospects

Where we go next

Our biggest technical limit points to our next direction, and the people we met point to the rest.

Reaching deeper soil

Mosses lack true roots, so they can only extract contaminants close to the soil surface, and metals in deeper soil layers stay out of their reach. To go deeper, we may explore engineering higher vascular plants such as ferns. An ecotype of the brake fern Pteris vittata collected in Kerala hyperaccumulates arsenic and chromium, with bioenrichment factors of up to 138 for arsenic and 235 for chromium.16 This suggests that Indian fern ecotypes carry the genetic machinery for multi-metal tolerance. Used as gene donors, such native hyperaccumulators could help us engineer deep-rooted, high-capacity vascular plants that reach deeper soil.

Working with what is native

Mr. Vinay Sahu asked us why we do not work with native species (Human Practices, Chapter 5). Native hyperaccumulators such as the Kerala fern ecotype are one answer: as gene donors, they would let us build on genetic machinery that already exists in Indian plants.

Arsenic in rice: what we will and will not do

Paderu showed us arsenic reaching people through rice. Clean biochar made from waste, as in Dr. Srivastava’s work, can reduce the arsenic rice takes up.6 That is a separate line of research from ours: our char would carry the metals our moss collected, so it is meant for water treatment, metal recovery and non-food land, never for fields that grow food.

Growing scientists where the problem is

With ITDA Paderu, a biotechnology laboratory and new curricula are being planned, so that students in the district can do synthetic biology themselves (Education, Phase 3). Students who can do science in their own district could one day study problems such as arsenic in rice themselves.

Commitments in progress

With MACUA, we are planning or carrying out work on mine-tailings case studies, mining careers and occupational hazards, and the accessibility of mining facilities, information and schemes for persons with disabilities. We will report outcomes only when they are complete and documented.

Questions we still have to answer

  • How much moss biomass grows per hectare, and how much metal it holds at harvest?
  • What leaching limit will the regulator apply to our char?
  • How does zinc behave during pyrolysis of our moss?
  • Can metal-rich char ever qualify for carbon credits?
  • What would a clean-up cost per hectare, against excavation?
