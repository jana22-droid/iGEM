Integrated Human Practices

We began with a question, not a solution: is heavy-metal contamination from mining really a problem? This page follows that question from our first reading, to the people who answered it, to the changes it made to Sanjeevani.

We first read how mining waste is governed and what it does to people’s health, in India and abroad. We then took our questions to a legal and environmental-justice panel and to occupational-health specialists, and went to mining sites in Odisha and to tribal communities in Paderu, Andhra Pradesh. Once we were convinced the problem was real, we went back to the science (which moss, which protocol, how to handle it) with bryophyte researchers, and asked a biochar researcher what should happen to the moss after it has done its job.

Along the way a conversation gave us our name, we changed a lab protocol, how we position Sanjeevani and the claims we make, and we planned what should happen to our moss at the end of its life.

Our approach

How we worked

We let each answer decide our next question, and we count a conversation as integrated only when we can name what it changed.

We kept our conversations in a shared interaction log: who the person is, what they told us, what we still needed to confirm, and what it meant for Sanjeevani. Each entry is marked as confirmed, still being filled in, or still needing attribution. On this page, an interaction appears as a change only when our records name one; the rest appear as context or open questions. Where our log gives a stage label, the card shows it.

Two kinds of sustainability guided the work. Environmental sustainability means contamination, tailings management and ecological restoration. Social sustainability means community perspectives, accessibility, gender equality, environmental justice and participation. Alongside them we held to an evidence-led approach: describe what Sanjeevani is meant to do for the environment, without overstating what it does for people’s health.

Chapter 1

Is it really a problem?

Yes. Before designing anything, we checked how big the problem is, what it does to people, and what the law does about it. Every record pointed to a real, present problem.

How big is it?

Toxic metals in soil are not a local problem. A 2025 analysis of 796,084 soil data points estimated that 14 to 17% of the world’s cropland, about 242 million hectares, exceeds agricultural thresholds for at least one toxic metal, and that 0.9 to 1.4 billion people live in high-risk regions.1

Mining is one route by which such metals reach soil and water. Arsenic, a Group 1 carcinogen in the IARC classification, occurs in soil and groundwater through natural processes and human activities, including mining, arsenic-based pesticides and irrigation with naturally contaminated groundwater.2

What it does to people

We started with the places where mining’s human cost is most visible: the lead-poisoning cluster around the open pit at Cerro de Pasco, Peru; mercury-based gold extraction at La Rinconada, Peru; and the acidic, metal-laden water of the Berkeley Pit in Butte, Montana, USA. In each case the contamination did not stay at the mine. It reached children, drinking water, food chains and nearby homes.

Closer to home, decades of opencast chromite mining in Odisha’s Sukinda Valley left unlined overburden dumps and exposed tailings. During the monsoon, rainwater leaches hexavalent chromium, a carcinogen, from these dumps into the Brahmani River. We would later visit Sukinda ourselves (Chapter 3).

How the law handles it in India

India regulates heavy metals from mining through overlapping environmental and mineral laws. The Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016 classify heavy-metal-bearing wastes such as ore tailings, slag and chemical sludge as hazardous. The Mineral Conservation and Development Rules, 2017 set how mining waste and overburden must be stored within a lease. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 requires mines to obtain consent to operate from State Pollution Control Boards and to treat acidic, metal-heavy wastewater before release.

Many of these rules followed disasters. The Sukinda crisis pushed stricter dump-containment rules into the 2017 Rules, and litigation over legacy mine waste led to frameworks for mapping and remediating contaminated ‘orphan’ sites. A series of ash-pond and waste-dam failures at industrial facilities, such as the 2019 Essar Power breach in Madhya Pradesh and the 2020 Reliance Power breach at Sasan, led to the 2021 fly-ash utilisation rules, a ‘polluter pays’ penalty of ₹1,000 per tonne of unutilised or improperly dumped ash, and annual safety certification for waste ponds.

The gaps mattered most for a project like ours:

  • Raw mine overburden is legally treated as ‘high-volume, low-effect’ waste, so it often escapes hazardous-waste treatment even when it holds leachable metals.
  • Official guidelines focus almost entirely on physical containment and chemical treatment. We found no specific protocols or safety standards for large-scale biological or genetic clean-up in mines.
  • When a company goes bankrupt or a lease expires, a mine can become an ‘orphan site’. Tracking of abandoned tailing dams is weak, and closure funds can be stuck in red tape for years.
  • Sand, gravel and other ‘minor minerals’ are regulated by individual states, whose environmental enforcement is often weaker.

Two findings stood out for Sanjeevani. The Environmental Impact Assessment Notification already asks for ‘progressive green-belting’ and the planting of hyperaccumulating local flora on stabilised dumps, so plants that take up metals already have a place in mine restoration. But the mine-waste guidelines we read contain no rules for living, engineered remediation agents, and a gap in the rules is not permission to act.

How other countries handle it

We compared how six jurisdictions regulate mining waste, and how five of them regulate releasing an engineered organism. The pattern was almost always the same: the law tightened after a disaster. Before Minamata disease, Japanese factories could legally discharge heavy-metal effluent into bays and rivers; afterwards, the Water Pollution Prevention Act of 1970 set binding national effluent standards for heavy metals. Brazil banned upstream tailings dams after the Mariana and Brumadinho collapses, and the EU’s mining-waste directive was drafted to prevent repeats of major tailings-dam failures.

The lesson was uncomfortable but important: knowing how mining-waste law works does not give anyone the right to introduce a living organism into a clean-up. Release is regulated separately, and a new species could create a new environmental problem by destroying or hybridising the native flora and fauna of a region.

Our answer

By the end of this reading we were convinced the problem is real. We were also left with questions no document could answer for us: who is responsible when a mine is abandoned, who gets a say in how a site is cleaned up, and what exposure actually does to the people who work and live there. So we took those questions to people.

What the panel changed

The panel changed the question we ask of Sanjeevani. Even a promising biological technology cannot, by itself, decide who is responsible for abandoned mine waste, how restoration is funded, how communities take part in decisions, or how environmental obligations are enforced.

The warning that bioremediation or CSR should not excuse a company’s environmental harm applies directly to us: a moss that cleans up after pollution could be held up as a reason to pollute. We take it as a test for how we describe Sanjeevani: as working alongside a company’s obligations, never in place of them. It fits the advice we received from Dr. Raghavan Gopalan to position Sanjeevani as supplementary rather than a replacement (Chapter 5).

The health panel

To understand what exposure actually does to people, we put our questions to occupational-health specialists: Dr. Ravi Babu K, Scientist-E at the ICMR-NIOH Regional Occupational Health Centre (Southern), Bengaluru, whose research covers biochemical toxicity in occupational exposure and biomarkers of exposure and response; and Dr. Shubhangi Pingle, who answered our questions in writing. Across these consultations, four lessons stood out.

  1. ‘Heavy metals’ are not one hazard. Lead can affect the central and peripheral nervous systems, blood formation, cardiovascular health, kidneys, bones and reproductive health, and it interferes with an enzyme involved in haemoglobin synthesis. Cadmium accumulates in the kidney’s tubules, disrupts calcium metabolism and damages lung tissue when inhaled. For chromium the chemical form matters: hexavalent chromium, Cr(VI), is more hazardous than trivalent chromium, Cr(III), with respiratory and skin effects and carcinogenic and mutagenic concerns. Nickel causes skin sensitisation and allergic contact dermatitis, and inhaled nickel dust or fumes may contribute to respiratory illness and certain cancers. Oxidative stress and the disruption of cellular processes through ionic mimicry or displacement are key mechanisms.
  2. Exposure and effect are measured differently. Biomarkers of exposure show that a person has been exposed: for example lead in whole blood for current exposure, or in teeth or bone for cumulative exposure; cadmium in blood and urine; chromium and nickel in urine. Effect biomarkers, such as urinary proteins and enzymes linked to cadmium’s kidney effects, show biological change. Choosing and interpreting them is work for qualified occupational-health professionals.
  3. Contamination, exposure and illness are different questions. Finding metals in the environment does not tell you how much a person has absorbed, and environmental sampling alone cannot establish a worker’s exposure or diagnose a health effect.
  4. Prevention comes in layers. Administrative controls (job rotation, training, housekeeping, maintenance), engineering controls (ventilation, isolation, enclosure), personal protective equipment and workplace hygiene are not interchangeable; the right mix depends on the site and its exposure assessment.

What the health panel changed

  • We widened Sanjeevani’s definition of impact to include the people who work in or live near affected areas, not only the land.
  • We do not claim that moss treatment prevents disease or reduces worker exposure. Such a claim would need measurements we do not have.
  • We treat exposure pathways separately: dust and airborne particles, contaminated soil and water, and direct contact can need different controls.
  • We present Sanjeevani as complementary to workplace controls, environmental monitoring and medical surveillance, never as a replacement for them.
  • The panel gave us a health-based reason to care about monitoring heavy-metal exposure and protecting workers and communities alongside remediation.

A point that stayed with us

Dr. Sruti Mohapatra, a disability-rights advocate and founder of Swabhiman, agreed with our concerns for persons with disabilities and for victims of occupational hazards in mining, and noted that only death counts are taken into account. Beside what the health panel taught us about chronic harm to kidneys, bones and lungs, her point is hard to forget: harm that does not kill is easily left uncounted. We have not yet turned it into a concrete change in Sanjeevani, and we list it among our open questions (Looking back). Our work with her is on our Inclusivity page.

Chapter 3

Going to the mines

Reading and listening were not enough, so we went to mining sites ourselves, and came back with a health question, a decision about scope, and a lesson in handling claims.

Sukinda Valley and South Kaliapani: Odisha Mining Corporation

We visited Odisha Mining Corporation’s mines in the Sukinda Valley and at South Kaliapani, the valley whose chromium contamination we had read about, and engaged with OMC at its headquarters. The visit grounded our questions about mining-related pollution, worker safety, waste management and land restoration in a real site.

Women in the mines

The women on our team went into the mines and inspected for themselves how the tailings are managed. We note this because Indian law long restricted women’s work in mines. The Mines Act, 1952 barred women from being employed in any part of a mine below ground, and above ground outside the hours of 6 am to 7 pm.3 Only in 2019 did a notification of the Ministry of Labour and Employment open below-ground technical, supervisory and managerial work to women during the day, and above-ground work, including in opencast mines, at night, with their written consent and safeguards.4

Chatrapur: Indian Rare Earths Limited (IREL)

At IREL’s OSCOM operations in Chatrapur, where minerals are mined from beach sands, we engaged with Suraj Prakash Sahu, CVR Murty, Ashish Kulshreshta and Pramod Kumar Mishra. The visit made us consider resource extraction, coastal environments and community benefits together, rather than reduce the issue to a simple opposition between mining and conservation. It also gave our project a health dimension: at Chatrapur we came across a reported link between the area and kidney disease.

How we handled it

The journalist Sandhya Ravishankar’s fact-verification method made us question and verify facts rather than repeat them. Our health panel explained why neither claim could settle the matter: the presence of contamination, the degree of human exposure and the presence of illness are related but different questions, and environmental sampling alone cannot establish exposure or diagnose disease.

What we concluded
  • Our project has not measured exposure or health outcomes at Chatrapur, so we have not verified either claim.
  • We do not say that beach-sand mining at Chatrapur causes kidney disease, and we do not say that it is harmless.
  • We do not use the kidney-disease link as evidence that Sanjeevani is needed there.
  • Settling the question would need exposure and clinical evidence, gathered and interpreted by qualified health professionals.

It changed how we write about health on this wiki: each health statement names its source and the kind of evidence behind it.

Tata Steel and Tata Steel Foundation: a decision to say no

Chapter 4

Paderu: a name, and a question about rice

A district collector suggested our name and sent us to Paderu, where we learnt that arsenic in soil can reach people through the food they grow.

Where our name comes from

D.K. Balaji, IAS, District Collector of Krishna district, Andhra Pradesh (district headquarters: Machilipatnam), suggested our name. Because we would be working with tribal communities, he advised a name that people could say and remember: Sanjeevani, rather than a coined name such as ‘Mosscraft’. In Indian tradition, Sanjeevani is the herb that restores life.

He also connected us with the Integrated Tribal Development Agency (ITDA), Paderu, so that we could verify the problem in a tribal area. He told us that arsenic had been found in rice harvested in the Paderu region.

What we found in Paderu

In Paderu we met local people, the District Medical and Health Officer and the principal of Government Medical College, Paderu. They told us that the arsenic comes from the soil itself, and reaches people through the rice grown in it. Treatment is offered locally, but some people do not take it because they do not trust the medicine.

This matched what we had read. Arsenic occurs in soil through natural processes as well as human activity, and rice is especially exposed because it is usually grown in flooded fields, which make arsenic in the soil more soluble and mobile, so the plants take up more of it.2

What we can and cannot say

  • We have not measured arsenic in Paderu’s soil, water or rice, and we could not find published measurements for the region. We report what we were told, and by whom.
  • We do not say that anyone’s illness in Paderu is caused by arsenic.

The question we brought back

We came back with a question about arsenic in rice. It took us to Dr. Sudhakar Srivastava of Banaras Hindu University, whose group studies biochar that reduces the arsenic rice plants take up, and that conversation gave Sanjeevani its end-of-life plan (Chapter 6).

Paderu also became the centre of our education work. With ITDA Paderu we ran an online programme and then an on-the-ground programme for school students, described on our Education page.

Chapter 6

After the harvest: what happens to the moss?

A moss that pulls metals out of soil becomes a concentrated, metal-laden waste. If we stopped at harvest we would only have moved the pollution.

The usual fates of such biomass are poor: landfill keeps the hazard, while open burning releases carbon as CO₂ and can volatilise metals.6

The question we brought back from Paderu took us to Dr. Sudhakar Srivastava of the Institute of Environment and Sustainable Development, Banaras Hindu University, whom we met when he spoke at the SynBio Conclave organised by iGEM IISER TVM. His group works on biochar made from waste. In a 2026 study, biochar from sweet-lemon and pomegranate peel reduced arsenic in rice shoots by 30 to 37% and in roots by 14 to 27%, while banana-peel biochar was less effective and raised root arsenic: the feedstock mattered.2

Talking with him gave us the idea of turning our own harvested moss into biochar. He explained how metal-laden moss could be handled as a biochar product, and how that product could be redistributed. We then built a plan from the literature and discussed it with him.

Our plan in brief (not yet tested on moss)

Dry the harvested moss and slow-pyrolyse it at 400 to 500 °C into biochar, a stable carbon solid; at that temperature, metals such as copper and iron stay in the char, while volatile cadmium must be caught from the off-gas.7 Test every batch for leaching, metal content and surface area, and send each batch to one use by its results: a sorbent for metal-laden water, metal recovery, or, only if it passes the leaching test, soil use on non-food land. Pyrolysis would also end the life of the moss, because no living cells survive 400 to 500 °C in an oxygen-free retort.

What our panels added to the plan

  • Who is legally responsible for the site, and what happens if the operator disappears?
  • Have the people who live and work there been informed, and given a say?
  • Who could be exposed at each step, and through which pathway?
  • Is the clean-up adding to a company’s obligations, or being used to excuse them?

We do not have full answers yet. Any honest deployment would have to answer them first.

Questions we have not closed

  • Chatrapur. Whether beach-sand mining there affects kidney health needs exposure and clinical evidence that we cannot produce ourselves.
  • Paderu. How much arsenic is in the region’s soil and rice, and what would help people trust the treatment they are offered. We have measured neither.
  • Uncounted harm. Dr. Sruti Mohapatra’s point that only deaths are counted: what should it mean for how Sanjeevani measures impact?
  • Other contaminants. Gram Vikas told us about fluoride in river water in industrialised Odisha. We have not worked out what, if anything, it means for a heavy-metal project.
  • Moss in the field. We do not yet know moss biomass yield per hectare or its metal load at harvest; both set the scale of our implementation plan.

Limits we state openly. Mosses reach only contaminants near the soil surface. We have no data yet on biochar made from moss. And we cannot say that Sanjeevani improves anyone’s health, so we do not.

For future teams

What we would pass on

Our method is reusable: start from the question, log what each conversation changed, and keep claims apart from evidence.

  1. Ask whether it is really a problem before asking how to solve it.
  2. Read the law early. It shows where a living solution would be regulated, and where the rules are silent.
  3. Invite people who may disagree with you. The sharpest point our legal panel made was a warning about technologies like ours.
  4. Go where people live with the problem. Paderu gave us a question that no paper had.
  5. Keep claims and evidence apart, and name the source of every claim.
  6. Plan the end of your organism’s life, not only its job.
  7. A ‘no’ can be a good project decision.
  8. Log every conversation with what it changed, and count only what you can name.
